What we found in Delhi's bus data

Our team's route from raw Delhi Transport Stack data to a crowding-aware journey planner. We started with the bus network and October 2026's e-tickets, then matched every ticket to a stop. From that we built per-route crowding and picked a demand model on a held-out week. Every step links to its full report and the script that builds it.

The story in five steps

Each step answers a question the next one depends on.

The bus network: what does the schedule tell us?

  • 2,554 routes, 6,812 stops, 65,322 trips and 2.39 million scheduled stop times, from two operators (DTC 1,619 routes, Department of Transport 935).
  • A clean feed: no orphan trips, no unknown or unused stops, every route has trips.
  • Trip starts peak at 07:00 and again at 13:00–16:00, and service runs round the clock.
  • The schedule is identical every day, and direction_id is blank. The timetable gives the network's structure, but crowding has to come from ticketing.

Who rides when: e-ticketing, October 2026

  • 2,559,899 tickets for 2.75 million passengers, 1–31 October, no duplicate rows.
  • Every ticket records both the boarding and the alighting stop. Loads along a route can be computed, not guessed.
  • Demand peaks at 09:00 and 18:00. Weekdays average about 91,000 tickets a day, weekends about 62,000.
  • Ticket routes match GTFS route names (99.9%). The vehicle number is missing on 48.5% of tickets.

Linking tickets to real stops

  • Ticket stop names are free text. Only about half match a GTFS stop name exactly.
  • Matching only against the stops each route serves (exact first, then fuzzy) resolves 97.9% of the 69,640 route-and-stop names, and 97.9% of tickets.
  • The remaining 2.1% stay unmatched on purpose: a wrong stop is worse than none.

Crowding along every route, hour and day type

  • The load on each stretch is boardings minus alightings along the GTFS stop order. It is scaled for ticket coverage and the buses scheduled that hour, against 60 riders per bus.
  • 89.1% of tickets are usable. 09:00 is the busiest hour network-wide, and the weekday morning peak is about three times Sunday's.
  • Each route-hour carries a confidence flag (few buses or few tickets means low). 8,610 of 20,739 route-hours are high confidence.
  • It flags 1,129 weekday route-hours where the busiest stretch is over capacity: a ranked list for operators.

Weekday, Saturday or Sunday? Choosing the demand model

  • We compared statistical models with gradient boosting on boardings per route, stop, hour and day type. They were tuned on 1–24 October and tested once on the held-out week of 25–31 October.
  • The hierarchical Poisson–Gamma model (B2) is about 27% better than the monthly average at the route-hour level the planner uses. Gradient boosting adds only about 3% at stop level, and nothing at route-hour level.
  • Under the gate set before the test, B2 serves the app: crowding is shown for weekdays, Saturdays and Sundays/holidays.

Live buses (GTFS-RT)

  • The real-time feed publishes about every 25 seconds. The 13 November snapshot held 2,483 buses.
  • The feed's route_id matches GTFS for 96% of buses, but its trip_ids don't, so the server places each bus on its route itself.
  • The live maps from src/decode_gtfsrt.py, src/track_bus.py and src/demo_live_map.py contain raw vehicle positions, so they aren't published here. The app shows live buses instead.
