Facts. One row = one mobile-ticketing ticket. 2,559,899 tickets span 2026-10-01 → 2026-10-31 (31 days). Every field is populated except vehicle_number (48% null); no fully-duplicate rows.

Facts observed in the data

  • Per the organizer, this mobile-ticketing channel is ~4–5% of total Delhi bus ticket sales — i.e. the file is a ~1-in-20 sample of ridership, not the full count.
  • Every ticket carries both start_stop_name and end_stop_name (both 0% null) — boarding and alighting stops are recorded.
  • route equals a GTFS route_short_name for 100.0% of tickets (99.9% of distinct route strings).
  • vehicle_number (present on 51.5% of tickets, 6,809 distinct) overlaps live GTFS-RT vehicle ids by 90.0%.
  • Ticketing volume is bimodal by hour (peaks 09:00 and 18–19:00) and higher on weekdays (91,183/day) than weekends (61,541/day).

Hypotheses derived from these facts

  • H1 — Because both stops are recorded, on-vehicle load is reconstructable directly as load = Σboardings − Σalightings along a route, with no alighting model. (Testable: load should return to ~0 at the terminus.)
  • H2 — The bimodal volume reflects commute demand, so on-vehicle crowding should peak at 09:00 and 18–19:00 and dip midday.
  • H3 — With route ≡ GTFS route_short_name, e-ticketing aligns to the GTFS network without a crosswalk.
  • H4 — The 90.0% vehicle-id overlap means ridership can be linked to live vehicle positions (for the 51.5% that carry an id).
  • H5 — The stop-name gap (below) is mostly formatting/abbreviation differences rather than missing stops, so it should be resolvable by name matching.
  • H6 — As a ~4–5% channel where 80% of tickets are AC and ticket_type_id is a single value, the mobile sample may over-represent AC / one fare product — its representativeness vs the whole fleet is worth checking before reading absolute volumes as ridership.
